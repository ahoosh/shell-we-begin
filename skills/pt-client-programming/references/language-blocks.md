## Symptom guide — standard (three columns)

| Zone | What you notice | Your next move |
|------|-----------------|----------------|
| **GREEN** | Comfortable or mildly uncomfortable. Movement stays controlled, and clicking is not painful or disruptive. Symptoms settle quickly, with no worsening later or the next morning. | Continue. After 2–3 green sessions, progress one small variable. |
| **YELLOW** | Symptoms rise more than expected; range, control, confidence, or quality decreases; compensation increases; or soreness remains the next morning. | Reduce range, resistance, repetitions, sets, speed, or time. Add support or return to the last successful version. |
| **RED** | Sharp pain, significant catching or locking, a painful pop, sudden loss of strength or motion, spreading symptoms, new numbness or tingling, or symptoms that do not settle after reducing the exercise. | Stop that movement. Contact {{clinician_first_name}} or another healthcare provider for guidance. |

Optional note: Noise by itself is not automatically harmful. A painless click with steady control may remain green; painful catching, slipping, or loss of function is red.

## Symptom guide — compact (two columns)

| | |
|---|---|

## Symptom guide — athlete with back/leg symptoms (criteria + plan)

| GREEN — Go as planned | YELLOW — Adjust | RED — Stop and get help |
|-----------------------|-----------------|-------------------------|
| Back or leg symptoms are 0–2/10. Symptoms are not moving farther down the leg. No new numbness, tingling, weakness, or giving-way. Walking looks and feels normal. You felt the same or better the morning after the last session. | Symptoms are 3–4/10 or your back feels guarded. Mild leg symptoms are present but not spreading. You are unusually tired, stressed, short on sleep, or more sore than usual. The last session left you mildly worse the next morning, but walking is normal. | Symptoms move farther down the leg. New numbness, tingling, weakness, or giving-way appears. Walking or posture changes. Pain becomes sharp, catching, rapidly worse, unusual, or 5+/10. |
| **Your plan:** do the planned session at an easy-to-moderate effort (3–5/10). Finish feeling as though you could do more. Progress only one thing at a time. | **Your plan:** reduce total work by about 30–50%. Use shorter intervals, walking, easy cross-training, or the reset routine. Do not add speed, hills, cutting, resistance, or extra sets. | **Your plan:** stop running, cutting, jumping, and loaded exercise. Use a comfortable position or gentle movement only if it settles symptoms. Tell a parent/guardian and contact {{clinician_first_name}} or your medical team. |

Check three times: before activity, during activity, and the next morning. If you are unsure, use Yellow. Numbers help, but symptom location, movement, and the next-morning response matter more than a single pain score.

## Adjust the dose — recovery is part of the plan

Your body adapts to the right challenge, and recovery is part of the process. A yellow day means changing the dose, not failure. Use less range, resistance, time, or support, then build again when movement, confidence, and the later/next-day response return to green.

Change one variable at a time: comfortable range, control, repetitions, resistance, complexity, then speed or activity-specific demand.

Total load includes rehabilitation, work, lifting, chores, travel, recreation, and sleep. A busy day may need a shorter routine.

Do not double the program after a missed day. Resume the usual dose that matches your current response.

If your reaching range decreases, return to the earlier supported version before loading farther.

## Weekly plan — scheduling notes

Categories may share a day. Use the lower end with yellow symptoms, fatigue, poor sleep, a busy week, or slower recovery. Use the upper end only with stable green responses and good recovery. Keep an easier day between demanding sessions. Do not double up after a missed day.

First-visit variant: These are provisional starting ranges because we have not yet established your home-exercise tolerance or recovery pattern. Start at the lower end while we learn your response.

## Phase lead-ins

Phase 1: Begin without added weight. These starting doses are guides, not targets to push through. Rest about 30–60 seconds between sets, or longer if needed. Full directions are in the Exercise menu.

Phase 2: Move into this phase when the starting exercises feel controlled and you have several green sessions in a row, including the next morning. Each exercise can progress at its own pace. Keep easier movement as a warm-up or recovery option; you do not need every version at every session.

Phase 3: Keep useful earlier exercises. Substitute the assigned ladder step for a related exercise. {{clinician_first_name}} will reassess tolerance and control before the loaded steps. Rest 60–90 seconds between sets or holds, longer if needed. Finish before fatigue changes control.

Choosing per day: Once more than five exercises are available, choose about five for the day.

## Ladder stop signals and progression

**Stop signals:** Stop for painful catching, slipping, sharp pain, new tingling, or sudden loss of strength. Do not push through loss of control. Return to the supported version and contact {{clinician_first_name}} if symptoms do not settle.

**Progression:** Advance after three green sessions with steady control and no worsening later or next morning. If daily activity is yellow, keep the loaded work light. Review the higher-demand variations with {{clinician_first_name}} later.

## Return-to-activity response

**Activity response:** Stay controlled during the activity, let symptoms settle later that day, and return to your usual baseline the next morning. If the response is yellow, shorten the next bout, lower the difficulty, or return to the prior stage. Use the red guide for catching, slipping, sharp pain, or sudden weakness.

## Daily checkpoints

| When | Ask | What it means |
|------|-----|---------------|
| Before | What colour am I? Where are symptoms? Is walking normal? | Choose today's starting plan. |
| During | Are symptoms rising, changing, or moving farther down? | Modify early. Do not wait for a large flare. |
| 2 hours later | Am I back near baseline? Is walking still normal? | A slow return toward baseline supports staying at the same stage. |
| Next morning | Better, same, or worse? Any new symptoms? | This check decides whether to progress, repeat, or reduce. |

## When a flare starts

| Time | Use this plan |
|------|---------------|
| First 24 hours | Stop the activity that increased symptoms. Change positions often; avoid staying in bed all day. Try 5–10 minutes of easy walking only if walking feels comfortable and symptoms do not spread. Use one gentle round of the reset routine; stop any movement that makes symptoms travel farther. |
| 24–48 hours | If symptoms are stable and not spreading, use the Yellow plan. Choose easy walking, pool, or bike; use about half of your usual time. Do not test speed, hills, cutting, or the movement that started the flare. |
| 48–72 hours | Return to the last level you completed comfortably, not the missed workout. Progress only after the later-that-day and next-morning checks are Green. Contact {{clinician_first_name}} if the flare is not settling, keeps returning, or includes new Red signs. |

**No make-up workouts.** A modified or missed day does not create a debt. Resume from the last level your body handled well.

## Build the week without overloading it

Running, sport, PE, hiking, and lower-body strength all count as load. Plan the total week, not each activity by itself.

| Part of the week | Dose | Guardrails |
|------------------|------|------------|
| Daily reset | 6–10 minutes, 1–2 times per day | Use a smaller selection on busy days. It should feel calming or neutral. |
| Impact sessions | 3–5 total sessions | A run or a sport session counts as one impact load. Do not add hard conditioning on the same day. |
| Strength | 1–2 sessions | Use 4–6 exercises. Reduce to 1–2 easy sets or skip entirely after a high-intensity practice or when fatigued. |
| Low-impact conditioning | 1–2 easy sessions | Pool, bike, or elliptical. Keep effort 3–5/10. |
| Full rest | At least 1 full day each week | No organised sport or make-up conditioning. Easy daily movement is optional. |

**Progression rules:** Move up only after two Green sessions at the same stage and a Green next morning. Increase one variable at a time: time, then speed, then hills or cutting. Add only 3–5 minutes when increasing total session time. Do not schedule more than two impact days in a row. Use no more than one organised sport in the same day. If travel, illness, poor sleep, or stress lowers recovery, use Yellow without guilt.

## Strength session rules (athlete)

Do two full sessions each week. Choose 4–6 exercises and stop with 2–3 good repetitions still "in the tank." Smooth control matters more than fatigue.

**How to progress:** First add 1–2 repetitions, then a third set, then a small amount of resistance. Change only one of those things in a session. On impact days, use no more than 2 sets of 6–8 and do not train to fatigue.

## Recovery signals

Sleep, regular meals, hydration, stress, and other health factors can all change how training feels. Notice patterns without judging them. A Yellow day is a useful adjustment, not a failure.

## Optional comfort tools

Warm shower or heat for 10–15 minutes if it feels good and skin sensation is normal. Massage tools on the lowest comfortable setting, 30–60 seconds per area, only on the areas {{clinician_first_name}} showed you. These tools are optional. They do not need to create soreness to be useful.

## Coach / family quick guide

- The next-morning response is the main progression check.
- Do not add conditioning to make up for a modified practice.
- Running and sport both count as impact; protect at least one full rest day.
- Allow the athlete to own the range, speed, and traffic-light choice.
- Competition return follows successful practice exposure and a shared decision with the athlete, family, coach, {{clinician_first_name}}, and the medical team.
